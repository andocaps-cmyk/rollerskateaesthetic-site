Your First Roller Derby Skates: What Derby Skaters Use

Written 7 October 2026 · updated 24 November 2026 · by Cass · written with an AI assistant

Of the 11 derby players we compiled from their WFTDA profiles, 7 name a metal competition plate, 4 give or use wheel hardness between 88A and 98A chosen for the floor, and 9 list protective gear in their setup; derby coach Dirty Deborah Harry adds a boot with little or no heel and a height-adjustable toe stop. So a first derby skate is a quad with an aluminium plate, 59-62 mm derby wheels around 93-96A for a sports-hall floor, an adjustable stop and full pads, and no player in our compilation tells a beginner which boot brand to buy. The players split on leather against carbon boots; both camps are named below.

What top skaters recommend

Features first, brands only where a skater’s setup shows a specific feature. Most derby setups here come from the WFTDA “Featured Skater” profiles of 2010-2016: they show what each player used at that date, not necessarily today, and a listed brand may be sponsored even when the profile does not say so.

Plate: aluminium, competition grade

  • Bonnie Thunders, the jammer often called the greatest player of modern derby (four World Cups, eight WFTDA titles with Gotham and Rose City; signature wheel with RollerBones), skated Powerdyne Reactor plates (WFTDA, 1 Aug 2010). Suzy Hotrod, Gotham jammer since 2004, 2008 WFTDA champion and Team USA, used the same plate (WFTDA, Nov 2011).
  • Scald Eagle, Rose City and Team USA jammer, WFTDA champion 2015 and 2016 (Bont gear; sponsorship not formally confirmed), used Infinity plates (WFTDA, 1 Oct 2015). Swede Hurt, Sweden’s MVP at the first World Cup and Gotham All-Star, and Snot Rocket Science, Team USA 2011 and 2014, both ran Roll-Line Killer plates (WFTDA, 18 Aug 2016; WFTDA, 4 Oct 2016). Juniper Simonis, 2015 WFTDA champion, used Crazy Venus plates; Satan’s Little Helper, three straight titles with Arizona and an international coach, used Boss plates (WFTDA, 10 Jul 2016; WFTDA, 10 Jun 2016).
  • That is 7 of 11 players on a metal plate. None of them gives a reason in the profile; the beginner reason comes from quad coach Lisa J, who says metal, never plastic (Boardwalk Skater, 2023).

Wheels: 88-98A, picked for the floor

  • Jackie Daniels, Windy City Rollers skater and coach who skated in the film Whip It, used 95A and 93A wheels (WFTDA, 1 Mar 2010). Bonnie Thunders’ RollerBones signature wheel is 62 x 38 mm in 94A or 96A (sponsor product; the figure comes from the product, not from her words).
  • Scald Eagle appears on a Bont blog post that says choose by hardness, not colour: 88-92A on polished concrete, 95-98A on a grippy wooden floor, control and stopping before speed for a jammer, softer wheels for blockers, and a narrow 59 x 38 mm format for acceleration (Bont, 14 Sep 2020; brand text, exact attribution of each point not verified).
  • Suzy Hotrod ran narrow “skinny” wheels and changed hardness with the floor.
  • Gap: no titled player in our compilation gives a hardness number aimed at a beginner. All of them link hardness to the surface.

Boot: low heel, and leather or carbon

  • Dirty Deborah Harry, coach behind Dirty School of Skate (about 299,000 subscribers; video filmed at Wicked Skatewear), says derby is skated low all the time, so look for a boot with no heel or a minimal heel (Dirty School of Skate, 21 Jun 2022). By contrast Carolina “Caro” Hernandez, an ex-derby bowl skater and Moxi rider, wants a heel for ramps, to climb transitions (Moxi, 7 Oct 2020): different disciplines, not a contradiction.
  • Disagreement, leather against carbon. Leather Riedell boots: Bonnie Thunders, Suzy Hotrod, Jackie Daniels (a cut-down model), Juniper Simonis. Carbon Bont or MOTA boots: Scald Eagle, Snot Rocket Science, Satan’s Little Helper, and Swede Hurt (moving to MOTA in 2016); Juniper was also moving to Bont. The newer profiles (2015-2016) lean to carbon. Nobody says which a beginner should start in.
  • Fit: Dirty Deborah Harry wants a boot that fits “like a slipper” and says go up a size if the width pinches, since your foot does not get longer.
  • Wide feet. Keep the low-cut boot: do not switch to a high-top recreational skate because it is sold as wide. Stockists say the Riedell R3 suits “standard to narrow feet” (Sucker Punch Skate Shop) and the Riedell Dart is “narrow at the toes” (Inline Warehouse). The Chaya Ruby (low-cut, truFIT last “medium to wide”) and the Crazy VXI (a toe box that widens) are listed for normal to wider feet; later, Bont makes its Hybrid derby boot in wide and double wide, to order. Try skates on, or ask your league's trainers, before you buy.

Toe stop: adjustable

  • Dirty Deborah Harry: a fixed “bullseye” toe stop marks an entry-level skate; a height-adjustable one gets more comfortable as you progress. Among the players, Snot Rocket Science and Juniper Simonis list separate aftermarket toe stops (Roll-Line, Bionic). Caro notes derby skaters are used to big toe stops.

Protective gear: full kit, every piece

  • Bonnie Thunders, Scald Eagle, Atomatrix (Oly Rollers jammer and 17-time world inline speed gold medallist; Atom sponsor), Swede Hurt, Snot Rocket Science, Satan’s Little Helper, Nina Erwes (Helsinki All-Stars captain, Big O 2016 MVP) Jackie Daniels and Smarty Pants (Pro Designed pads) all list protective gear in their setup; Scald Eagle adds wrist guards and hockey elbow pads, Snot Rocket a mouthguard (WFTDA, 8 Sep 2011; WFTDA, 8 Sep 2016).
  • Estro Jen (Michelle Steilen, Moxi founder; a park skater, not a derby player) ranks them for beginners: helmet, then wrist guards, the most common skating injury, then knees and elbows (Estro Jen, 9 Jan 2021). The skater behind this site gives no order for a beginner: every piece matters, and you leave none of them out.
  • What WFTDA rules require (mouthguard included) and how derby pads differ from recreational ones: roller derby protective gear.

Train before you upgrade

  • Suzy Hotrod: the same gear will not make you her level, “practicing hard will”. Atomatrix puts fitness, strength and technique first; Smarty Pants, Team USA co-captain and coach since 2003 (Antik and Pro Designed sponsor), says get fit before you start (WFTDA, May 2013). Swede Hurt says skate as much as you can and listen; Jackie Daniels says learn the fundamentals.

From the skater behind this site

  • Size: follow the brand’s own chart and advice, including “go up a size” when the brand says so; sizes vary a lot between makers. Measure in millimetres, standing, at the end of the day.
  • Tight means the fastenings, not the size: laces and straps done up firmly; loosening is never his advice.
  • Wheels: 95A and up on a rink floor, 78A-85A outside. That fits the derby range above for an indoor hall.
  • Quad caveat: the skater behind this site learned on quads but has skated inline for most of his life and does not skate quads now, so weigh his quad points with care.

What people who skated them report

These are the reports we could find and read from people who say they actually used them, with their limits. We did not use Amazon customer reviews, which Amazon does not allow sites to reproduce.

  • Sure-Grip Rock GT50: Ninja Beam, a roller derby skater on the channel Down and Derby Talk, skated it for about nine months as a new derby player (video from about 2020). Very comfortable at first, with thick padding, but the padding compressed over time and caused heel lift; the stock cushions and 95A wheels were very hard and the toe stop poor, all worth replacing early on polished practice floors. Her verdict: a sound budget skate for the first year of derby, which most players outgrow. (source, watched 24 Oct 2026)
  • Sure-Grip Rock GT50, second report: Nottingham Roller Derby’s blog (2 Apr 2014) gathers two of its skaters’ experience: one wore them for 13 months from her beginners’ course and found that, with new wheels and cushions, they could last years; the other found them very comfortable and more secure than the Riedell Volt. The blog notes a fit suited to normal-to-wide feet, very hard (about 95A) wheels that make first stops easier but grip less, and a nylon plate that flexes, so heavier skaters may want metal sooner. (source, read 24 Oct 2026)
  • Riedell R3: J.K., a derby skater who bought a pair, writes on the blog Try Not To Fall! (2 Mar 2014) that the boot runs narrow but is comfortable, leaves half an inch to an inch of toe room, and is hard to remount a better plate on; the stock wheels are worth replacing indoors but work well outside. J.K. expects most skaters to move to a leather boot after about a year. An older model year. (source, read 24 Oct 2026)
  • Riedell Dart: we found no review from someone who skated it that we could cite. The derby advice blog Derby Gear Advice (15 Jul 2012) answered “no” to whether it is a good beginner skate, pointing to a vinyl boot that stretches, very hard cushions and wheels, and a plastic plate known to bend; this is advice, not a test, and it describes the 2012 model, which had a plastic plate, whereas the current Dart has an aluminium plate. (source, read 24 Oct 2026)
  • Triple Eight Certified Sweatsaver Helmet: Neil Hester, a skateboarder (not a roller skater) who reviews his own gear on YouTube, had worn one for at least six months and bought a second (video from about 2024). He found the fit very snug once he picked the right size from his head circumference, with a spare set of thicker pads in the box to adjust it, and calls it the most comfortable helmet he has tried. The catch is the “sweat saver” brow pad: for a skater who sweats a lot it soaks up sweat and then drips into the eyes, so he wrings it out during sessions; the side pads go in the washing machine, but he advises against machine-washing the centre pad, whose fabric came unstuck. After several falls where his head hit the ground, the shell showed no damage. (source, watched 24 Oct 2026)
  • Triple Eight Saver Series pad set: Angel Ann Lyte, a beginner roller skater on YouTube, bought the set on Amazon and tried it on when it arrived (video from 2020); she did not skate in it in that video. The wrist guards felt solid, with hard plates; the elbow pads were loose on her small elbows; and on one pad the Velcro flap began to pull away the first time she opened it. She planned to repair it herself. (source, watched 24 Oct 2026)

Beginner FAQ

What should my first roller derby skates have?

Among the 11 derby players we compiled, 7 used a metal competition plate and those who give wheel figures sit between 88A and 98A, chosen for the floor. Coach Dirty Deborah Harry adds a boot with no heel or a minimal heel, because derby is skated low, and a height-adjustable toe stop. Add a helmet and full pads.

What wheel hardness do derby skaters use?

Jackie Daniels used 93A and 95A, Bonnie Thunders’ signature wheel is sold in 94A and 96A, and a Bont post featuring Scald Eagle gives 88-92A on polished concrete and 95-98A on a grippy wooden floor. Suzy Hotrod changed hardness with the floor. All of them tie hardness to the surface.

Leather or carbon boot for derby?

The players are split. Bonnie Thunders, Suzy Hotrod, Jackie Daniels and Juniper Simonis skated leather Riedell boots in their profiles; Scald Eagle, Snot Rocket Science, Satan’s Little Helper and Swede Hurt had moved to carbon Bont or MOTA boots by 2015-2016. No player in our compilation tells a beginner which to buy.

What protective gear do I need for roller derby?

Nine of the 11 derby players list protective gear in their setup: helmet, knee pads, and for several wrist guards, elbow pads and a mouthguard. Estro Jen, a park skater, ranks them helmet first, then wrist guards, knees, elbows; the skater behind this site gives no order for a beginner: every piece matters, and you leave none of them out. Your league’s own gear rules come first. See the protective gear checklist. See roller derby protective gear for the five pieces WFTDA rules require.

Can I use my outdoor skates for derby practice?

Dirty Deborah Harry says wheels are swappable: keep the skate and change to harder wheels indoors. The skater behind this site gives 95A and up on a rink floor and 78A-85A outside. See outdoor vs indoor wheels.

Does expensive gear make you a better derby skater?

No. Suzy Hotrod says the same gear will not make you her level, practising hard will; Atomatrix, Smarty Pants, Swede Hurt and Jackie Daniels all put fitness, technique and practice first.
