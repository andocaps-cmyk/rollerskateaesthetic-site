The 3 park and bowl skaters in our compilation who describe their setup (Estro Jen, Carolina “Caro” Hernandez and Kid Ace) all ride hard wheels, an aluminium plate and a supportive boot, and Estro Jen tells a beginner to go to the park with wheels of 92A or harder, in 62 mm rather than 57 mm. What top skaters recommend

Features first, brands only where a skater recommends a brand for a specific feature. Where a skater works for or owns the brand they talk about, we say so.

Wheels: hard from the first session, 62 mm to start

  • Estro Jen (Michelle Steilen, founder of Moxi Skates and a leading figure of quad park skating; her signature wheel is the RollerBones Bowl Bomber) tells beginners to ride hard wheels at the skatepark from the start, for feel on changing curves, and not to bring soft “gummy” outdoor wheels: 92A or harder (Estro Jen, 9 Jan 2021).
  • Diameter. For a beginner she would pick 62 mm over 57 mm: more speed for less effort while you cannot pump well yet. 57 mm gives more feel for tricks, once you pump. She rides mixed herself, 57 at the back and 62 at the front (Moxi, 31 Jan 2021; her own wheel, in both sizes).
  • What the pros actually ride. Kid Ace, Moxi team rider who skates vert, gaps and stairs, rides 101A for speed (Moxi, 25 Sep 2020). Caro Hernandez, Colombian bowl skater from the Concrete Is Beautiful generation and Moxi rider, rides narrow 103A wheels so they do not bite when she slides, and would like a slightly smaller diameter (Moxi, 7 Oct 2020).
  • Disagreement on diameter. 62 mm for a beginner and for speed (Estro Jen; Kid Ace on speed) against 57 mm or smaller for feel (Estro Jen for skaters who already pump; Caro wants smaller). Both camps agree on hard.
  • Wheels are the swappable part. Dirty Deborah Harry, coach behind Dirty School of Skate (about 299,000 subscribers), says any pair of skates will do at first and you change wheels for the place: hard indoors, soft outdoors, hard at the skatepark (Dirty School of Skate, 21 Jun 2022).

Boot: stiff, with a heel

  • Caro, a former derby player, cannot ride a soft boot on ramps: she uses a stiff boot with a heel, which spreads her weight and helps her climb transitions faster (Moxi, 7 Oct 2020).
  • Kid Ace rides a stiffer version of a Moxi boot; for her a supportive boot is essential to jump and land hard (Moxi, 25 Sep 2020).
  • Tia Pitman (“Steeez T”, winner of Concrete Waves, Australia’s first invitational quad contest, 2023; pro for Chuffed Skates, which sells her signature boot) rides a firmer boot for ankle support (Chuffed, product page, undated; S1, 3 Nov 2023).
  • Side by side, not a contradiction: Dirty Deborah Harry prefers a boot with no heel or a minimal heel for derby, where you skate low; Caro wants a heel for ramps. Different disciplines.

Plate: aluminium, and mind its length

  • Caro and Kid Ace both ride an aluminium competition plate with narrow trucks; Kid Ace says hers has never broken despite hard use. Caro runs medium-hardness cushions at the park and softer ones on the street; she notes other skaters prefer soft in the bowl (only one detailed opinion on cushions).
  • Kid Ace felt more stable and faster after moving to a longer plate, and says to choose plate length with care. No one in our compilation gives a beginner a length in numbers: that is a gap.

Toe stop and slide blocks

  • Caro, used to big derby stops, mounts a small, short-stem toe stop close to the plate so it does not catch on the ramp; Kid Ace caps the size of her stops and wears them out fast.
  • Both ride slide blocks (grind blocks) under the plate; Caro likes a rounded shape that centres the weight, in a material that slides without wax, and adds grind trucks for coping.
  • Estro Jen tells beginners to master the toe stops: run on them, then fall back onto the knee pads (Estro Jen, 9 Jan 2021).

Protective gear: helmet, wrists, knees, elbows

  • Estro Jen ranks them: helmet (the most important), wrist guards (the most common skating injury), knee pads, elbow pads; and learn to fall (Estro Jen, 9 Jan 2021). The skater behind this site gives no order for a beginner: every piece matters, and you leave none of them out.
  • Slam, founder of Chuffed Skates (brand voice, no verified competition record; recommends S1 and RollerFit), wants a certified hard-shell helmet, basic pads to start and high-end pads for heavy park use, and pads neither too tight nor slipping (Chuffed, 18 Aug 2022).
  • Caro wears a hard-shell helmet and pro knee pads; Tia Pitman the same make (both featured on the helmet brand’s own blog: S1, 6 Dec 2021).

From the skater behind this site

  • Size: follow the brand’s own chart and advice, including “go up a size” when the brand says so. Measure in millimetres, standing, at the end of the day.
  • Tight means the fastenings, not the size: laces, straps and buckle done up firmly — it matters more when you land.
  • Hardness and surface: 78A-85A outside, 95A and up on a smooth floor; a smooth surface lets a hard wheel roll fast, a grainy one sends every bump into your feet. Skatepark concrete is the smooth case.
  • Quad caveat: the skater behind this site learned on quads but has skated inline for most of his life and does not skate quads now, so take his quad points with care; the park advice above is the park skaters’.

Beginner FAQ

Can I take my outdoor quad skates to the skatepark?

You can take the skate, but change the wheels. Estro Jen tells beginners not to bring soft, gummy outdoor wheels to the park and to use 92A or harder from the first session; Dirty Deborah Harry also says hard wheels at the skatepark and notes that wheels are the easy part to swap. See outdoor vs indoor wheels.

Should a beginner get 57 mm or 62 mm park wheels?

62 mm, according to Estro Jen: more speed for less effort while you cannot pump well yet. She keeps 57 mm for skaters who already pump and want more feel for tricks. Kid Ace also rides hard wheels for speed.

Do I need a stiff boot for the skatepark?

The park skaters we compiled all ride a supportive one. Caro Hernandez uses a stiff boot with a heel to climb transitions faster, Kid Ace needs a stiffer boot to land jumps, and Tia Pitman's signature boot is firmer for ankle support. Dirty Deborah Harry notes ankle support exists in entry-level skates too.

What protective gear do I need at the skatepark?

All of it, from the first session. Estro Jen ranks it helmet first, then wrist guards (the most common skating injury), knee pads and elbow pads, and tells beginners to learn to fall onto their knee pads. The skater behind this site gives no order for a beginner: every piece matters, and you leave none of them out. Slam of Chuffed Skates recommends a certified hard-shell helmet. See the protective gear checklist.

Do I need slide blocks and a small toe stop as a beginner?

Not on day one, but they are what park skaters add. Caro Hernandez and Kid Ace both ride slide blocks; Caro mounts a small, short-stem toe stop close to the plate so it does not catch on the ramp, and Kid Ace caps her toe-stop size for the same reason.
