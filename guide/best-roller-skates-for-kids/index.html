Best Roller Skates for Kids: What Coaches Recommend

Written 7 October 2026 · updated 24 November 2026 · by Cass · written with an AI assistant

The best roller skates for kids fit now and adjust over several sizes, rather than being bought big, and have a brake the child can reach. That is what the coaches and skate schools we found agree on, along with a helmet and pads from the first session. Quad or inline is the coaches’ open question.

The 3 named coaches and skate schools we found who give buying advice for children (Skip Clinton of DevaSkation, Alex Shulgan of InMove Skates and the Skatefresh school in London) agree on three things: buy a skate that fits now, ideally one that adjusts over several sizes rather than one bought big; make sure it has a brake the child can reach; and put on a helmet and pads from the first session. Two more sources (a Swiss Federal Office of Sport teaching sheet and Exeter Roller Derby’s junior pack) back the pads and the brake, for five in all. None of them is a titled star skater, and two of the three are shops that sell what they recommend: we say so on every point.

What do coaches recommend in kids’ roller skates?

Features first, no brand: none of the coaches recommends a specific children’s skate for a feature it alone has. Skatefresh lists brands with no specific reason, so we leave them out.

Size: fit now, adjust later — do not buy big

  • Skip Clinton: at most one size up to allow for growth, never more, because a skate that is too big hurts feet and ankles; no extra pair of socks to fill the gap; competition skates fitted from the start. His words: “not more than one size bigger” (DevaSkation, 5 Jan 2022, updated 3 Oct 2024).
  • Alex Shulgan: measure both feet in millimetres (trace the foot on paper, heel to longest toe) and compare with the chart of that exact model, not with the child’s age. Many children’s models adjust over 3 or 4 sizes (InMove, 3 May 2023, updated 9 Aug 2026).
  • Skatefresh: for children, skates with an adjustable boot that covers several sizes (Skatefresh FAQ, updated 4 Oct 2026).
  • Nuance: Clinton tolerates one size of margin; Shulgan wants the exact measurement and lets the adjustable boot provide the margin.

Brake: one the child can reach, and learn to stop first

  • Skatefresh: on inline skates, always a heel brake — “always buy skates with a heel brake”; on quads, toe stops are fine (Skatefresh FAQ).
  • Shulgan puts an accessible brake among his priorities, with a supportive boot and cuff and laces plus a buckle. Clinton suggests upgraded toe stops for better stopping.
  • Gabi Schibler (Swiss Federal Office of Sport): braking and stopping come before everything else (mobilesport.ch, Jan-Feb 2021, PDF in French).

Wheels: softer for small children and rough ground

  • Clinton: softer wheels for little ones, fewer jolts and more grip. Shulgan: pick hardness by surface — soft for grip, hard for durability; indoor and outdoor wheels differ. Exeter Roller Derby: hard for speed, soft for grip on a slippery floor (Exeter Roller Derby junior pack, undated).
  • No source gives a hardness number for children.

Quad or inline: they disagree

  • Clinton: quads first for young beginners, for an easier balance point; inline needs more ankle strength and suits older children. The skater behind this site disagrees on balance: inline skates are the more stable of the two, standing still and rolling, because the inline boot holds the ankle stiff and the wheels become an extension of the leg. Quads turn tighter; that is manoeuvrability, not stability. See inline skates are more stable.
  • Skatefresh accepts both (heel brake on inline, toe stops on quads). Schibler only covers inline. Shulgan covers both without choosing.
  • For the general question, see quad vs inline: which to buy first.

Protective gear from the first session

  • All five sources ask for it. Skatefresh: a helmet is compulsory (a bike helmet is acceptable), plus at least wrist guards and knee pads, or no lesson. Clinton: helmet always, knee and elbow pads strongly advised, wrist guards and a mouthguard too. Shulgan: helmet and pads, above all while learning.
  • Schibler on fit: the helmet sits two fingers above the root of the nose with straps done up tight; wrist guards with the plate on the palm side; knee and elbow pads, all well fitted.
  • Exeter Roller Derby (juniors who play derby): a helmet to WFTDA/MRDA standards and a mouldable mouthguard are compulsory.

Age: from toddler to six, depending on who you ask

  • Clinton: from the time a child walks, ideally 3 to 5 with instruction (DevaSkation kids FAQ, 22 Sep 2020, updated 20 Dec 2020). Schibler: basic inline learning from 4. Skatefresh: private lessons from 6, 4 on request.

What to avoid

  • Clinton advises against department-store skates for regular use. Exeter Roller Derby asks juniors for derby or speed skates, not “jam” or shoe-style skates, with ankle support that does not lock the ankle. Skatefresh suggests schools hire before they buy (Skatefresh schools).

Parent FAQ

Should I buy my child's roller skates a size bigger?

Skip Clinton says one size up at most, never more, and no extra socks to fill the space. Alex Shulgan prefers to measure the foot in millimetres against the model's chart and let an adjustable skate, which often covers 3 or 4 sizes, give room to grow. Skatefresh also recommends an adjustable boot. See should you size up roller skates.

Are quad or inline skates better for a child?

The coaches disagree. Skip Clinton suggests quads for young beginners, for an easier balance point, and inline for older children with more ankle strength. The skater behind this site disagrees on balance: inline skates are more stable than quads, standing still and rolling, because the boot holds the ankle; quads turn tighter, which is manoeuvrability, not stability. Skatefresh accepts both, Alex Shulgan covers both without choosing, and the Swiss Federal Office of Sport sheet only teaches inline.

What age can a child start roller skating?

Skip Clinton says from walking age, ideally 3 to 5 with instruction; the Swiss Federal Office of Sport sheet starts inline basics at 4; Skatefresh gives private lessons from 6, or 4 on request.

What protective gear does a child need?

A helmet always; Skatefresh also requires at least wrist guards and knee pads, and Skip Clinton strongly advises elbow pads too. The Swiss sheet asks for the helmet two fingers above the root of the nose with tight straps. See the protective gear checklist.

Do kids' skates need a brake?

Skatefresh says always buy inline skates with a heel brake; toe stops are fine on quads. Alex Shulgan lists an accessible brake among his priorities, and the Swiss sheet teaches braking and stopping before anything else. See heel brake, toe stop or jam plugs.
