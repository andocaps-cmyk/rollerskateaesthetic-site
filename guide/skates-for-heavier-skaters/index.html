Yes, many roller skates have a weight limit, so read the maker's figure before you buy: Impala tests its skates to 220 lb (about 100 kg), and some brands stop at 250 lb. Bigger skaters do skate on standard gear: former Moxi team skater Shove rides a reinforced nylon plate daily at over 350 lb.

We found 3 recognised skaters and coaches who talk about gear for bigger bodies (coach Dirty Deborah Harry, former Moxi team skater Shove, and K Woodward of Arch Rival Roller Derby), and the one point they make most clearly is practical: check the weight limit the maker publishes, and expect to hunt for knee pads above 2XL. On the plate they disagree with the shops: Shove rides a reinforced nylon plate daily at over 350 lb, while shops recommend aluminium. Published weight limits we could read cluster around 100 kg (220 lb); they are shown separately below as manufacturer data. Weight limit: read it before you buy

  • Shove asks skaters to check the weight limit, noting that some brands stop at 250 lb (Moxi, 5 Mar 2021).
  • No recognised skater gives a weight figure of their own. The published limits are in the manufacturer section below.

Plate: nylon or metal? A real disagreement

  • Shove contradicts the idea that a nylon plate must break under a heavy skater: she rides a reinforced nylon plate every day at over 350 lb without breaking it, and keeps a metal plate for the park. In her view breakage depends more on how hard you skate (aggressive skating, jumps) than on body weight: “it’s not about how much weight the plate can hold” (Moxi, 5 Mar 2021).
  • Shops say metal. Lou’s Skate Garage, a Melbourne skate workshop, says alloy plates break less under a heavier skater and nylon flexes (via Princess on Skates, undated). SkatePro, a retailer, recommends aluminium for a heavier build but says nylon can work if the product’s weight limit allows it (SkatePro, undated). These are shops with something to sell, not recognised skaters.
  • Where they meet: Shove and SkatePro both say the weight limit is what to check. Shove’s point is one skater’s experience; no other recognised skater in our compilation tested it.

Wheels: with a hub

  • Shove found hubless wheels harder to roll at her weight and prefers wheels with a hub; she suggests replacing the stock wheels of the Moxi Beach Bunny, and notes you push harder on rough ground (Moxi, 5 Mar 2021). Only one recognised voice on wheels.
  • Two wheel sellers add a hardness point: RollerGirl.ca suggests going one step harder above about 200 lb and choosing wide, rigid hubs (RollerGirl.ca, undated); Derby Warehouse says slightly harder and alloy hubs over nylon hubs (Derby Warehouse, July 2019). They disagree on how much harder; no recognised skater gives a number.

Protective gear: large sizes are rare

  • K Woodward found no hard-cap knee pad in XXXL when she started; she wore soft work knee pads, then ordered custom ones. Pad sets (knee, elbow, wrist in one size) fit badly because the three areas are not the same size, and sellers’ “2X” often runs small (Queer Girl Straight Skates, 29 Oct 2019).
  • Shove says pads above 2XL are hard to find and often custom-made; she names one range that fits her, the 187 x Moxi “thick” set, made with the brand that published the video (Moxi, 5 Mar 2021).
  • K Woodward’s ways to find gear: ask a local shop to order large sizes, try on the skates and pads of a league mate your size, and look online for skaters your size and what they ride.

Before the first session

  • Dirty Deborah Harry: quad trucks come “factory tight”; loosen them before your first outing or the skate will not turn. That is her only technical point on the skate itself (Dirty School of Skate, 19 Jan 2021).
  • Her other advice is about skating, not gear: practise getting up from a fall, first without skates, then with them, in whatever way suits your body (a “tripod” on your hands); weight over the front trucks, do not lean back; first session in a small, clean, enclosed space.
  • Shove adds: lace up sitting on the floor with the foot tucked, or standing and bent over; get up using a wall or a knee and toe stop.

Inline skates: one extra voice, not counted

  • OlderBladers, a small inline channel whose author calls himself “not an authority” and says he weighed over 300 lb for years, prefers a firm liner, a rigid shell and cuff, an aluminium frame and aluminium-core wheels, and avoids very soft or low-cut skates above about 200 lb (OlderBladers, 28 Jan 2022). Bladeville, a retailer, also suggests a stiff boot and aluminium frame (Bladeville, 28 Dec 2022). No recognised inline skater in our compilation covers this topic.

What we could not find

  • No recognised skater on bearings, boot width or boot stiffness for heavier quad skaters, and no published weight limit for Sure-Grip, Powerdyne, Chaya or Moxi plates.
  • No titled competitor (rink, park or speed) talking about gear for heavier skaters.

From the skater behind this site

  • Size: follow the brand’s own chart, measure in millimetres, standing, at the end of the day.
  • Tight means the fastenings, not the size: laces, straps and buckle done up firmly.
  • Hardness and surface: 78A-85A outside, 95A and up on a smooth floor. He has no specific advice by body weight.

Beginner FAQ

Will a nylon plate break under a heavier skater?

Sources disagree. Shove, a former Moxi team skater who says she weighs over 350 lb, rides a reinforced nylon plate every day without breaking it and keeps a metal plate for the park; she says breakage depends more on how hard you skate than on weight. Shops (Lou's Skate Garage, SkatePro) lean towards aluminium. Everyone agrees on one thing: check the weight limit the maker publishes. See skate plates and frames.

Do roller skates have a weight limit?

Some makers publish one. Impala says its skates are tested to 220 lb (about 100 kg); Decathlon gives 100 kg for its Fit100 and Fit500 inline skates under the European standard EN 13843, whose adult class runs from 20 to 100 kg. Rollerblade's FAQ gives no limit. Shove says some brands stop at 250 lb. Check the product page before you buy.

What wheels should a heavier skater use?

Only one named skater speaks to it: Shove found hubless wheels harder to roll at her weight and prefers wheels with a hub. Two shops that sell wheels (RollerGirl.ca, Derby Warehouse) suggest going slightly harder and choosing rigid or alloy hubs. No coach in our compilation gives a hardness number for heavier skaters. See the wheel hardness tool.

Where can I find knee pads above 2XL?

Both Shove and K Woodward of Arch Rival Roller Derby say they are hard to find: K Woodward found no hard-cap knee pad in XXXL when she started and ended up with custom pads. She advises asking a local shop to order large sizes, buying knee, elbow and wrist pads separately rather than as a set, and trying the gear of a league mate your size. See protective gear that actually fits.

Is there anything to adjust on new skates before the first session?

Coach Dirty Deborah Harry says quad trucks come factory tight and should be loosened before your first time out, or the skate will not turn. She also suggests practising getting up from a fall, first without skates, then with them, in a small, clean, enclosed space. See how to get up.
