Skate Bearings: What Top Skaters Say Actually Matters

Written 7 October 2026 · updated 24 October 2026 · by Cass · written with an AI assistant

Of the 20 skaters, coaches and shop specialists in our compilation who talk about or list their bearings, the five who give a beginner advice all say the same thing: the bearings that come with your skates are enough, and what matters is keeping them clean, not the number on the box. The pros themselves ride anything from ABEC 5 to ABEC 9, and the only exotic bearings (ceramic, titanium) belong to downhill racers sponsored by the brands that make them. Spend on the boot and the wheels first; spend on bearings, if ever, once you can hear that yours are worn.

What top skaters recommend

For a beginner: keep the stock bearings

  • Viktor Hald Thorup, a two-time Olympic speed skater on ice and Rollerblade World Team member who coaches on YouTube (he also owns a skate shop, topskate.dk), tells beginners that ABEC 7 or 9 bearings are enough, and to clean them after skating in the rain (Viktor Thorup, 25 Jul 2023). In his own setup video he rides ABEC 9 and says good bearings will not make you win, but bad ones are a pain (Viktor Thorup, 21 Apr 2021).
  • Casper, presenter of Powerslide’s official channel (Powerslide staff; he says he worked four years in a skate shop), says to keep the bearings the skate comes with, and to choose the skate for its boot rather than its wheels (Powerslide, 11 Mar 2024).
  • The Thisissoul skate shop channel (Amsterdam; a seller, presenter not named) says the stock wheels and bearings are enough at the start (Thisissoul, 20 Sep 2020).
  • Asha, coach at the London skate school Skatefresh, does not single out bearings, but warns against expecting gear to do the work: in her words, “technique is the most important thing” (SkatefreshAsha, 21 Dec 2022).
  • On quads, Kelly, an instructor at Skate Base London (local reputation), lists ABEC 7 bearings in her adult beginner setup (Skate Base London, 9 Sep 2024).

What the pros actually ride: ABEC 5 to 9

  • ABEC 5 at the top of aggressive skating. Chris Haffey, X Games gold medallist (2009, 2011), had ABEC 5 bearings on his Remz pro model (sponsor Remz) (Be-Mag, 23 Aug 2011). Si Coburn, a UK blading figure since the 1990s who runs the Rush Skatepark academies (Rollerblade, Ground Control), rides ABEC 5 and tells skaters to check their screws regularly (Be-Mag, 29 Oct 2019).
  • ABEC 7. Antony Pottier, Winterclash pro winner 2018, rides his sponsor Seba’s ABEC 7 bearings (Be-Mag, 19 Jun 2016).
  • ABEC 9 or the brand’s own “9” scale. Nick Lomax (IMYTA champion 2006), Léandre Fabri and marathon skater Sheila Juan Franco (3rd at the 24h Rollertour) all list Wicked ABEC 9, from their sponsor Powerslide (Lomax, Fabri, Juan Franco, Powerslide team pages, undated, read 7 Oct 2026). Slalom world champions Lorenzo Guslandi and Lorenzo Demuru, and Alexandre dos Santos, ride Twincam ILQ 9 (Guslandi, Demuru, dos Santos, Powerslide team pages, undated); Kim Sung Jin, world no. 3 in 2010, had the same on his Seba signature skate (Rollerenligne, 11 Jan 2011).
  • Speed: Swiss bearings, from sponsors. Bart Swings, world champion and five-time Berlin marathon winner (Powerslide/Matter), rode Wicked Swiss bearings in 2017 (Big Wheel Blading, 9 Oct 2017); Felix Rijhnen, world champion 2017, rides the Wicked series that carries his name (Big Wheel Blading, 26 Oct 2017). Neither gives a reason in terms of features.
  • Ceramic and titanium: downhill, sponsored. French downhill skater Etienne Herreros rides ceramic bearings from his sponsor EOskate (Big Wheel Blading, 2017); Spanish downhill skater Noel Jarillo rides titanium bearings from his sponsor CSP (Big Wheel Blading, undated). Neither recommends them to beginners.

Maintenance: never let them run dry

  • Alexandre Lebrun, president of the International Inline Downhill Association, says bearings should never run dry (Rollerenligne, 15 Feb 2013, updated 3 Nov 2020). Thorup: clean after rain (above).

Where the skaters differ, and what nobody says

  • A number or no number. Thorup and Kelly still give a rating (ABEC 7 or 9; ABEC 7). The skater behind this site says the rating tells you nothing about speed (below). They agree on the practical point: a beginner does not need to buy bearings.
  • Gaps. No top skater in our compilation gives a cleaning method or a cleaning interval. The quad park and derby skaters list bearing brands but give no bearing advice. Nobody compares ceramic with steel for an ordinary skater.

From the skater behind this site

  • ABEC is a machining tolerance, written for industrial spindles turning at speeds no skater reaches. It says how precisely the bearing was made, not how fast you will roll, and the box does not claim otherwise. ABEC 9 on a cheap bearing is a sales argument. More in bearings: the number on the box means nothing.
  • What changes your roll is keeping grit out and re-oiling: a clean ABEC 5 outrolls a dirty ABEC 9. Wipe your wheels after an outdoor session, and open the bearings when they start to sound dry, not when they stop turning. Step by step: one drop of oil, not two, with the four-object tool kit.
  • Wet skates: he says he does not know whether today’s bearings are stainless, so he dries his skates before they go in a bag, just in case. See never bag wet skates and can you skate in the rain.
  • Spend on the wheels before the bearings: 78A-85A outside, 95A and up on a rink floor. See outdoor wheels: what to buy and the wheel hardness tool.

Beginner FAQ

Do I need better bearings on my first skates?

No. Casper (Powerslide), the Thisissoul shop channel and Olympic speed skater Viktor Thorup all say the bearings that come with a decent skate are enough to start, and nobody in our compilation tells a beginner to upgrade bearings first.

Does a higher ABEC number make skates faster?

Not by itself. ABEC is a machining-tolerance rating; it does not say how fast you will roll, according to the skater behind this site. Thorup says ABEC 7 or 9 is plenty and that good bearings will not win you anything, while bad ones are a pain to live with. Pros in our compilation ride anything from ABEC 5 to ABEC 9.

What ABEC rating should a beginner look for?

If a number is printed at all, ABEC 7 or 9 is what Thorup calls enough for a beginner, and Kelly, an instructor at Skate Base London, suggests ABEC 7 on a beginner quad. Two aggressive pros in our compilation, Si Coburn and Chris Haffey, rode ABEC 5 in their published setups.

How often should I clean my bearings?

When they start to sound dry, not when they stop turning, says the skater behind this site (method on cleaning your bearings); wipe your wheels after every outdoor session. Thorup says clean them after skating in the rain, and Alexandre Lebrun of the International Inline Downhill Association says never let them run dry.

Are ceramic or titanium bearings worth it for a beginner?

Nobody in our compilation recommends them to a beginner. The only skaters riding them are downhill racers sponsored by the brands that sell them: Etienne Herreros (EOskate ceramic) and Noel Jarillo (CSP titanium).

Can I skate in the rain without ruining my bearings?

Thorup says clean them after rain. The skater behind this site says he does not know whether today's bearings rust, so he dries his skates before bagging them, just in case.
