Will I fall when I learn to skate after 50?

Yes. I do not believe anyone can learn to roller skate without falling, at any age, and I will not tell you otherwise. What I can tell you is where falls tend to land, and how to learn so that you never fall alone.

The falls you take while learning are the only preparation for falling. A beginner cannot go fast, so those falls are small and slow. A fall lasts too short a time to decide anything in it, and those are not decisions you can practise: you need to have been through falls already, so that a fast one holds as few surprises as possible. Someone who learned without ever falling meets their first real fall on the day they are going fast.

Where you land when you fall on your own

  • Forwards, the knees, the elbows and the hands take it: knee pads and wrist guards are there for this fall. With your wrist guards on, putting your hands out in front is the basic advice, to keep your head off the ground, and it comes by instinct anyway.
  • Backwards, you land on your tailbone and the back of your head. This is the fall that hurts, the one nobody expects, and elbow pads are there for it too.
  • On the hip, very rarely when you fall alone. You mostly end up on your side by rolling along the ground after the fall, or when another skater, a pedestrian or a car hits you.
  • Falls are so varied that you have to stay careful anyway.

Never learn alone, at any age

My advice is the same at 20 and at 60: never learn on your own. Better still, learn with a teacher, or at a rink or a paid, supervised venue where there is help on hand if something goes wrong.

Whether a fall is more dangerous at your age is a medical question, not a skating one, and I am in no position to answer it. Two of the coaches below say to talk to your doctor before you start.

A Canadian public-health report on 962 inline skating injuries found that about half came from losing control or balance with no obstacle involved, and that the most common injury was a fracture, most often of the forearm or wrist. Its data come mostly from children’s hospitals, so it says little about skaters over 50 (Public Health Agency of Canada, read 10 Nov 2026).

Protective gear: the one point all agree on

  • Helmet, wrist guards, knee pads: Liz Miller, Bill Begg and Jamie L. all list them. Liz Miller and Jamie L. add elbow pads, and Liz Miller shows how to put pads on correctly. Her line: “It's not really a matter of if you fall, but when you fall” (Get Rolling FAQ, undated).
  • Hip protection, a side note: Liz Miller suggests padded hockey shorts worn under loose shorts, or crash pads, for hips and tailbone; Bill Begg specially recommends hip crash pads for older skaters (Inline Planet, 24 Jul 2013); Jamie L. mentions padded clothing. The only study we read on hip protectors, a Cochrane review from 2014, followed people aged 78 to 86 on average who fell while walking: a small drop in hip fractures in care homes, no effect shown for people living at home, and no skaters at all (PubMed, read 10 Nov 2026). Hip padding makes most sense against a collision; a skater who falls alone rarely lands on the hip.
  • Dirty Deborah Harry’s part 1 gives no pad list; her sponsor sells soft knee braces, which are not impact pads.

Boot and ankle support

  • Liz Miller calls good ankle support essential for older skaters and warns against cheap plastic boots; for skaters with strong calves she suggests low-cut quad skates instead (Get Rolling FAQ, undated). Jamie L. also asks for good ankle support.
  • Bill Begg says nothing on skate type, wheel size or brake.

Skates that sat in a cupboard for years: check them first

  • Dirty Deborah Harry advises against skates kept for decades: aged leather, hardened cushions, worn bearings — even if older leather boots and metal plates were often better made than today’s cheapest skates. Her phrase: “You might need to buy a pair of skates that is worthy of you” (Dirty School of Skate, “Roller Skating Over 40 Part 1”, 24 Sep 2026).
  • For inline skates, the skate shop Slick Willies lists what long storage does: the plastic of the shell loses its flexibility and can crack, foam and glue can crumble with damp, and polyurethane wheels can harden and crack after years unused. It gives no set number of years (Slick Willies blog, read 10 Nov 2026).
  • From the skater behind this site: my own skates, unused for several years and badly stored, crumbled in my hand when I picked them up; the half-plastic, half-foam trim around the boot fell apart. Plastics and foams are what age worst, and wheels can harden too. Good bearings, kept oiled, are what holds up best.

Wheels, brake, surface

  • Liz Miller: soft wheels (under 78A) on rough asphalt, and a heel brake mounted on the side of your dominant hand (Get Rolling FAQ, undated). She is the only source on both points, and does not tie them to age.
  • Bill Begg: start on perfectly flat ground (Inline Planet, 24 Jul 2013).

A skate aid at first, and how to progress

  • Dirty Deborah Harry suggests a rolling skate aid (a walker-style trainer), touched as little as possible, to get past the fear of falling; she names one brand, which we could not confirm as a sponsor. She also advises getting the body moving first (walking, stretching), skating regularly even once a week rather than long sessions, and only skating forwards at first (Dirty School of Skate, “Roller Skating Over 40 Part 1”, 24 Sep 2026).
  • Slow, careful progress is common to all four sources.

Which skates should an older beginner choose?

Helmet, wrist guards, knee pads and elbow pads before the skates, and a teacher, a rink or a supervised venue for the first sessions; then a skate with real ankle support, quad or inline. If a part of you is afraid of falling, Dirty Deborah Harry’s skate aid suggestion is the next thing to try.

Beginner FAQ

Can I learn to roller skate without falling?

No. Expect to fall while learning, at any age. Never learn alone: take lessons, or skate at a rink or a supervised venue where help is on hand.

Am I too old to start roller skating at 50 or 60?

None of the coaches we compiled says so. Bill Begg, a speed skating coach, writes that age is only a number for a skater in reasonably good health, Liz Miller teaches students around 65 to 70, and Dirty Deborah Harry, 59 in her video, coaches skaters over 40. Liz Miller and Jamie L. both say to talk to your doctor first. Whatever your age, expect to fall while learning, and do not learn alone.

What protective gear should an older beginner wear?

Helmet, wrist guards and knee pads at the very least, according to Liz Miller, Bill Begg and Jamie L.; Liz Miller and Jamie L. add elbow pads. At the start every pad matters, with no ranking. Liz Miller and Bill Begg also suggest hip padding, such as padded shorts or crash pads; the one study on hip protectors involved people aged 78 to 86 who fell while walking, not skaters, and a skater falling alone rarely lands on the hip.

Should I start on quad skates or inline skates after 50?

There is no common answer. Jamie L., a rink instructor, suggests quads for balance; Liz Miller and Bill Begg are inline coaches and do not compare the two; Dirty Deborah Harry coaches quads and does not compare either. What they do agree on is good ankle support (Liz Miller, Jamie L.).

Can I use my old roller skates from decades ago?

Check them first. Dirty Deborah Harry advises against skates that sat in an attic for decades: leather that has aged, hardened cushions and worn bearings. Plastics and foams are what age worst, and wheels can harden; good bearings kept oiled hold up best. No source gives a set number of years.

Is a skate trainer or walker worth it for an older beginner?

Dirty Deborah Harry suggests a rolling skate aid at first, touching it as little as possible, to get past the fear of falling. She is the only coach in our compilation who mentions one, and her video is sponsored (by a brace and compression brand, not the aid).
