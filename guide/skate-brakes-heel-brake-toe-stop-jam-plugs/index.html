Skate Brakes: Heel Brake, Toe Stop or Jam Plugs?

Written 7 October 2026 · updated 24 October 2026 · by Cass · written with an AI assistant

Of the 13 skaters and coaches in our compilation who talk about stopping, none tells a beginner to buy skates without a brake: on inline, Skatefresh coach Asha says keep the heel brake until you can stop at least one other way; on quads, coach Dirty Deborah Harry and rhythm teacher Dunbeezy both say pick a height-adjustable toe stop over a fixed one. Jam plugs replace the toe stop only in specific rhythm and jam styles, and the downhill skaters add one rule above all: learn to slow down on the flat, at low speed, before any slope. The skater behind this site disagrees with the coaches on two points, how useful the heel brake really is and whether a toe stop brakes at all; both sides are set out below.

What top skaters recommend

Inline: keep the heel brake while you learn other stops

  • Asha, coach at Skatefresh, the London skate school (no sponsor known; she sells her own online lessons), says keep the heel brake until you have mastered one, preferably three, other ways of stopping (Skatefresh, 2 Feb 2021).
  • Jan Welch, editorial director of Big Wheel Blading with 30 years in the industry (owner of the wheel brand Dream Urethane), lists a brake among the features of a first inline skate, next to 80 mm wheels and a rigid boot (Big Wheel Blading, updated 14 Apr 2026).
  • Dustin Werbeski, street, big-wheel and off-road rider (Xsjado, Powerslide, Undercover), says learning to stop is harder than learning to start, and to begin off-road on grass or packed dirt (Big Wheel Blading, 27 Jul 2017, updated 8 Jun 2023).
  • Danny Aldridge, Rollerblade urban rider, puts starting, the T-stop and tight turns first, before the city, whose ground changes from cobbles to grates to granite (Rollerblade, 22 Oct 2018).
  • Chloé Seyrès, world freestyle slalom champion (Seba team in 2011), says take lessons to learn how to fall and how to brake, with pads on (Rollerenligne, 31 May 2011).

Quads: an adjustable toe stop, not a fixed one

  • Dirty Deborah Harry, coach behind Dirty School of Skate (about 299,000 subscribers; the video was filmed at Wicked Skatewear, and its description says it backs no brand), says look at the stop: a fixed “bullseye” stop marks an entry-level skate, and a height-adjustable one gets more comfortable as you progress (Dirty School of Skate, 21 Jun 2022).
  • Dunbeezy, who teaches rhythm skating on YouTube (reputation off YouTube not verified), gives three checks for a cheap skate that is still good: leather boot, aluminium plate, adjustable stop, “the difference between levels in skates are those three features” (Dunbeezytv, 12 Aug 2024).
  • Estro Jen (Michelle Steilen, Moxi founder; brand content) tells first-time park skaters to get really comfortable on their toe stops, running on them and then falling back onto knee pads, and lists the T-stop among the basics in her beginner-mistakes video (Estro Jen, 9 Jan 2021; Moxi, 29 Apr 2026).

Toe stop size and height depend on where you skate

  • Carolina “Caro” Hernandez, bowl skater and Moxi rider (video published by Moxi), runs a small, short-stem toe stop mounted close to the plate on her park skates so it does not catch on the ramp; a former derby skater, she was used to big stops. On her street skates she sets the stop lower, to push and stop quickly (Moxi, 7 Oct 2020).
  • Kid Ace, Moxi team rider (vert, gaps and stairs by her own account), uses the largest-diameter stops her skate takes and says she wears through them a lot (Moxi, 25 Sep 2020).

Jam plugs: a style choice, not a beginner default

  • Dunbeezy describes the regional rhythm styles: Chicago (JB) skaters and the jam and Atlanta speed-boot styles use jam plugs in place of a stop, while Detroit skaters keep a full toe stop (Dunbeezytv, 12 Aug 2024). He started on a low speed-style boot with jam plugs himself.
  • Gap. No skater in our compilation recommends jam plugs to a first-time skater, and none explains how to stop without a toe stop. Every coach quoted above who talks about quads starts beginners with a stop.

Before any hill: slow down on the flat first

  • Etienne Herreros, world no. 3 in downhill time trial in 2017 (EOskate, RollerN’co), says, in his words, “Learn to slide on flat ground at low speeds first”, and not to start on fast slopes (Big Wheel Blading, 2017).
  • Noel Jarillo, Spanish championship runner-up at 16 (Flying Eagle, Roex BCN, CSP bearings), says learn to slide gradually on small slopes (Big Wheel Blading, date not shown).
  • Gap. Neither downhill skater names a brake type; in speed and downhill, no skater in our compilation talks about brakes at all.

Disagreements

  • Is the heel brake worth keeping? Asha says keep it until you have other stops. The skater behind this site (an inline street skater, not a coach) almost never used his: when he did, it was either not strong enough to stop him or it hurt the front of his shin, and once you are a little trained it gets in the way of crossing one foot over the other in turns. He does not say beginners should go without it: “it is there for a good reason”. Both views are given here on purpose, as he asked.
  • Does a quad toe stop brake? The coaches above teach toe-stop control as a basic. The skater behind this site says the quad toe stop is useless for braking: rocking onto it lifts the wheels off the ground, so it is a point that does not roll, to hold still, pivot or push from, not to slow down. See the toe stop is not a brake.

From the skater behind this site

  • The plough stop comes first. Feet a bit wider than the hips, heels pushed out, toes in, knees bent, press down and out: it works on quads and inline, needs no brake at all, and both feet stay on the ground. See the plough stop.
  • He stops with a T-stop, on inline, and the back leg pushes hard; if it feels effortless, you are not slowing down. See toe stop drag vs T-stop and the T-stop is a slow-down, not an emergency stop.
  • On a real hill, you do not brake. When a slope is too steep, at any level, braking halfway is more dangerous than riding it out: no panic, wheels straight, knees bent, wait for the flat. Knowing how to brake standing still is not knowing how to brake under stress. Beginners start on very small, short slopes. See hills you do not brake.
  • On wet or slippery ground, he sets the front stops (quads) or the heel brake (inline) down first.

Beginner FAQ

Should a beginner take the heel brake off inline skates?

Not yet, according to Skatefresh coach Asha: keep it until you can stop at least one other way, preferably three. The skater behind this site rarely used his, finding it weak or hard on the shin, but he does not tell beginners to remove it. See the heel brake: two honest opinions.

Is an adjustable toe stop better than a fixed one?

Yes for most quad beginners, say coach Dirty Deborah Harry and rhythm teacher Dunbeezy: a fixed bullseye stop marks an entry-level skate, and an adjustable stop can be set to suit you as you progress. Caro Hernandez sets hers low for street and small and close to the plate for the park.

Should I buy jam plugs instead of a toe stop?

Not as a first setup. In our compilation, jam plugs appear only in specific rhythm and jam styles (Chicago, Atlanta, jam, per Dunbeezy), and no skater recommends them to a first-time skater.

Which stop should I learn first?

The skater behind this site starts with the plough stop: it works on quads and inline, needs no brake and keeps both feet on the ground. Danny Aldridge (Rollerblade) puts the T-stop among the basics before the city. See the plough stop and T-stop first, then stepping plough.

Can I brake my way down a hill?

Do not count on it. Downhill skaters Etienne Herreros and Noel Jarillo say learn to slide on the flat or on small slopes first. The skater behind this site says that on a slope that is too steep, braking halfway is more dangerous than riding it out to the flat. See hills you do not brake.

Does the quad toe stop slow you down?

Coaches such as Estro Jen teach toe-stop control as a basic, but the skater behind this site says it does not brake: rocking onto it lifts the wheels, so it is a point to hold still or pivot on. See rock onto the toe stop to hold still or pivot.
