What are flat, rockered and anti-rocker frames?

Flat: all wheels the same size and all touching the ground. Rockered: the middle wheels sit lower (or the end wheels are smaller), so the skate rolls on a curve and pivots more easily. Anti-rocker: an aggressive setup with big wheels at the front and back and small, hard wheels in the middle that leave room for grinding. A tri-rocker, ridden by Andrew DiPaolo, sits between the two.

In between: Andrew DiPaolo (Razors, Eulogy) rides a tri-rocker and says more wheels on the ground means control and speed. (Be-Mag, 25 Aug 2017)

Where they agree. Spassov and Lino, the only two who address beginners directly, set out the trade-off the same way: anti-rocker is easier to grind; four equal wheels turn more easily, go faster and absorb jumps better, but grinding asks more. Their shared advice is to try both and choose.

Urban and fitness: flat is the default

  • Asha skated 3×110 mm on a flat frame for years for speed and comfort on rough city ground, then moved to 4×90 mm on a slightly rockered frame for “wizard” flow. Her beginner advice: pick wheels for the skating you will do most, not for what she uses; a rockered frame does not give you technique, and “technique is the most important thing”.   • In another lesson she says both setups work for flowing transitions, with different technique; see a rocker is a style, not a requirement.

What no one in our compilation says

No speed or long-distance skater discusses rocker, and no aggressive pro beyond Spassov and Lino gives beginner frame advice: Haffey, Julio, Lomax and others publish edits, not “what to buy” videos. No one tells a newcomer to buy a rockered fitness skate.

Which frame setup should a beginner choose?

  • City, fitness, learning to skate: a flat four-wheel frame, as most skates ship. Asha and Klaudia both say gear does not replace technique. A strongly rockered banana frame is a slalom setup, not a city one.
  • You want to try slalom: start on the skates you have; when tricks begin, a banana rocker (smaller end wheels) is Klaudia’s first change, and a frame that switches between flat and rockered keeps both uses.
  • You want to grind: anti-rocker is the easier start according to Spassov and Lino; flat is a fair choice too, and they say to try both. Start with the first aggressive skates guide.

Why start on a flat frame, and how sure is that?

Starting on a flat frame is what almost every skater does, and the mechanics point the same way. On a flat frame all four wheels touch the ground; on a rockered frame only two do, so the skate’s support from front to back is shorter. The retailer SkatePro describes the rocker as a setup for experienced skaters that can feel unstable and harder to control for a beginner, and the flat frame as the more stable, easier one. Wikipedia calls flat the setup for speed, stability and long distance, fitted on most non-aggressive skates. We found no study that compares people who learned on each.

The opposite claim exists. Rockin Frames, a site that sells rockered frames, says beginners learn faster on a rocker because it does not punish mistakes, and it cites no study. The claim cannot be checked, because almost nobody learns on a rocker. My own reasoning starts from a question: who has ever learned on rockers, to be able to say so? Very few people, I think, and only learning on one would settle it.

What the sources say about rocker, frame length and wheel size

I have never learned on a rocker, never really skated a strongly rockered frame, never skated frames of very different lengths and never skated big wheels. The points below come from the sources named, read on 10 November 2026, not from my own skating.

  • Stability at speed: a strongly rockered (“banana”) frame, with only two wheels on flat ground, is harder to keep stable at high speed and more prone to wobble in a straight line, according to Wikipedia. Rockin Frames, which sells rockers, also says banana rockers turn fast but are unstable at high speed.
  • Turning: a rocker turns more easily and more tightly than a flat frame (Wikipedia; Rockin Frames).
  • City or slalom: extreme banana rockers are made for freestyle slalom, and Rockin Frames itself says they are not for urban skating; Wikipedia describes the flat frame as the one for speed, stability and long distance.
  • Frame length: a long frame spaces the wheels for stability at high speed, which is why speed skaters use long frames; hockey and slalom skaters use short frames because they are more manoeuvrable (Wikipedia, inline skate frame).
  • Wheel size: bigger wheels, such as 110 mm, raise your centre of gravity and make the skate harder to control for a beginner; SkatePro advises 80 or 90 mm to start.
  • Anti-rocker: with only the front and back wheels rolling, the skate is slower, less stable and harder to turn; it is the setup most used by aggressive skaters (Wikipedia). It is outside my own skating.

From the skater behind this site

  • Inline and quad are different skates: rocker talk applies to inline frames. On quads the plate pivots on its trucks instead; see quad vs inline.
  • On rough ground, what I can speak to is wheel hardness, not frame shape: 78A-85A outdoors, 95A and up on a rink floor. Check with the wheel hardness tool.
  • Size by each brand’s own chart; tightness is for the fastenings, not the size. Use the size calculator.
  • Leave the frame where the factory put it until you have edge control: frame shifts come after edge control.

Beginner FAQ

Do I need a rockered frame as a beginner?

No. No skater in our compilation tells a beginner to buy a rockered frame. Coach Asha (Skatefresh) says a rockered frame does not give you technique; slalom skater Klaudia says your first 10 to 20 tricks need no special gear. Starting flat is what almost every skater does, and the mechanics point the same way: on a rocker two wheels touch the ground instead of four, so the skate's support from front to back is shorter. We found no study that compares learning on each.

Is anti-rocker or flat better for learning to grind?

The pros split 7 for anti-rocker and 5 for flat. Bobi Spassov and Ricardo Lino, who give beginner advice, say anti-rocker is easier to grind, while four equal wheels turn more easily and absorb jumps better; they both say to try both.

What is a banana rocker?

A slalom setting with smaller wheels at the front and back, for example 76 mm ends around 80 mm middle wheels. Klaudia recommends it as the first setting once you start slalom tricks.

What frame do slalom champions use?

A short rockered frame. Lorenzo Guslandi rides a 225 mm rockered frame with 84 mm/86A wheels and Lorenzo Demuru a short rockered 3x90 frame for style; all compiled slalom skaters are sponsored, mostly by Powerslide.

Can I rocker my city skates?

Klaudia recommends frames that switch from flat to rockered in minutes so one skate covers city and slalom. Asha moved from a flat 3x110 setup to a slightly rockered 4x90 for flow, but says to choose wheels for the skating you do most.

Should I move my frames?

Not at first. The skater behind this site and coach Asha Kirkby leave the frame where the factory put it until you have edge control.
