Who Writes Roller Skate Aesthetic, and How

Written 23 August 2026 · updated 9 November 2026 · by Cass · written with an AI assistant

Hi — I'm Cass. I skate inline, outdoors, and this site is the guide I wanted when I started and could not find. Here is where everything on it comes from, and what it deliberately does not try to be.

Who I am, and what I'm not

I'm a skater, not a coach. I have no certificate, no competition record and no club behind me, and I'd rather say that on the first page you read than let you find out later. What I do have is years of the ordinary kind of skating this site is about: streets, traffic, bad tarmac, hills that turned out to be steeper than they looked, and rain that arrived halfway home.

Cass is the name I write under. It is the name on the site, on the Pinterest account and on the inbox, and it is the only one you'll need.

Where the inline material comes from

Anything here about inline skates comes from riding them. How a hard boot holds your ankle, why loosening your laces makes things worse, what a real hill does to your judgement, which part of a wet road is actually dangerous, what it feels like when the adrenaline arrives before the skill does. None of that is repeated from another site. Where my experience and a popular piece of advice disagree, I say so on the page and I say why.

That also means the limits are mine. I don't race, I don't skate parks or ramps, and I don't teach children. Those pages don't exist here, and that is on purpose.

Where the quad material comes from

I learned on quads, but I have skated inline for most of my life and I don't skate quads any more, so I don't pretend to. Quad technique on this site comes from Najete Kada, a French champion in artistic roller skating and a Flaneurz ambassador, who teaches quad in a club. Around thirty pages name her in the text, and her material is marked as quad every single time. Her authority is quad; nothing she says is presented here as a claim about inline skates.

How a page gets written

Each page answers one question and says, in its own words, what it does not cover — the terrain it excludes, the skill level it isn't for, the thing you should look up elsewhere. That line isn't filler. A page that hasn't worked out its own edges is a page that will confidently answer a question it was never qualified to answer.

Every page carries a visible date, taken from the same record machines read, so the two can't drift apart. Skating technique doesn't go stale quickly, but you shouldn't have to guess how old a page is.

Every page is written with an AI assistant, and every page says so — in the line under its title and again at the bottom. The assistant does the writing. What it writes from is my own notes on skating: what a hill does to your judgement, why loosening your laces makes things worse, which stretch of a wet road is the dangerous one — and, for quads, Najete's teaching. It is not allowed to invent a skating fact, and where it finds a popular piece of advice that contradicts my notes, my notes win. The illustrations are generated with AI as well.

What this site doesn't do

  • It isn't medical advice. Pages about pain are about boots, fit and socks. If something still hurts once the skates are off, see someone qualified.
  • It isn't a shop, and it doesn't review things I haven't looked into. Some pages carry Amazon affiliate links, marked as such, and I earn a commission if you buy through one — at no extra cost to you. Pages with nothing worth selling carry no links, and one page here argues against buying a whole product category.
  • It isn't a substitute for a lesson. Someone watching you skate beats any page, including mine.
