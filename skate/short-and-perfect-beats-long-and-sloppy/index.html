There is a trap waiting for every new skater, and it is not a lack of courage or a lack of practice. It is a lack of legs.

The correct skating position is a half-squat held continuously, and it asks for strength in muscles you have not used this way before. As a beginner you simply do not have it yet. So you hold the position, your thighs start burning, your knees creep up, your backside drifts back — and you finish the session skating in exactly the shape you were trying to get rid of.

That is the trap: the last twenty minutes of every session are spent rehearsing the fault. And since what you repeat is what sets, the long sessions are actively teaching you the wrong thing while you congratulate yourself on the effort.

So invert it. Do a few strides properly, then stop. Half a dozen, with your body upright and your knees genuinely over your toes, is worth more than half an hour of the tired version.

One detail that decides whether this works: resting means standing still. Rolling along in a slumped position is not a rest, it is more repetitions of the fault. Come to a stop, let your legs recover, then go again — and film a few of those strides from the side so you know which version you were actually doing.

The strength arrives on its own from there, because the position is what builds it. Inline technique, from Asha Kirkby of Skatefresh, an instructor certified by the Inline Certification Program. She teaches inline skating, and that is the discipline this page is about.
