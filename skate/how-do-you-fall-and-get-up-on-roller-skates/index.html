How Do You Fall and Get Back Up on Roller Skates?

Written 22 August 2026 · updated 13 November 2026 · by Cass · written with an AI assistant

Fall forwards, onto knee pads and wrist guards, not backwards. Quad teacher Najete Kada turns the fall into a slow descent: squat to the floor, put your hands down, stretch your legs out in front of you. To get up, roll onto your knees, plant one skate flat in front of you, press both hands on that knee and push.

Which way should you fall on roller skates?

Forwards. Falling is a skill with a direction. A forward fall lands on equipment designed to take it, and you can see it coming. A backward fall lands on your tailbone or the back of your head, and you cannot. Practise it on carpet or grass, in full gear, from standing, and never alone. Keep it slow: the point is to have been through a few falls at walking pace before you ever fall at speed.

How do you fall safely on roller skates?

Slow the fall down instead of trying to avoid it. That is how Najete Kada, a French champion in artistic roller skating, teaches it to quad beginners. From the safety position — knees bent, hands resting on your knees — lower yourself slowly into a squat until you touch the floor, put your hands down, and stretch your legs out in front of you. That is a descent.

How do you get back up on roller skates?

Getting up is not improvised either. Turn over onto your knees, bring one skate flat under you so the knee points up, press both hands on that knee, and push up with your weight over your feet. It is the position a knight takes, and it works because your hands do part of the lifting.

Which pads do you need for falling?

All four. None of this is optional equipment. Helmet, wrist guards, knee pads and elbow pads are not a ranked list for a beginner: wrist guards take the forward fall, elbow pads are what you land on going backwards, and knee pads are what let you practise a fall on purpose — slowly, on a soft floor, never alone — instead of fighting it.

What this page does not cover: this covers the ordinary fall you take while learning on flat ground. It does not cover falling at speed, falling on a hill, or what to do about an injury — and it is not first-aid advice.
