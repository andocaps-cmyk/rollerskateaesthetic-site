How to Fall on Roller Skates: Learn It by Falling, Slowly

Written 22 August 2026 · updated 24 November 2026 · by Cass · written with an AI assistant

You will fall, and the first time, a list of steps won't help much: a fall is over too fast to follow one, and you panic. That is normal. What helps is falling small and often while you are still slow. Coaches teach it as a drill: fall on purpose, slowly, on a soft floor, in full gear, never alone, until going down becomes a reflex. Until then, your pads take the fall, so wear all of them from day one.

Why the steps don't work the first time

I'm a street skater, not a coach. I learned outside, and I fell the way most people fall: by accident. The professional advice is further down and you should use it. But I think you should hear this first.

You can't learn to skate without falling. The good news is hidden in the bad: when you start, you can't go fast, so your first falls are small ones. That is the time to learn how to fall, because those are the falls you can afford.

A fall is over in a moment. There is no time inside it to decide anything, and you can't rehearse decisions you won't have time to make. The first time, you panic, and nobody can explain anything to someone who is panicking. That is why a fall broken into steps on paper, read the night before, rarely survives your first real one.

What you are really learning is the fear. You learn to handle it by living through falls, small ones first, so that the day you fall at speed, it isn't the first time. If you have never fallen and your first fall comes fast, you have nothing to go on.

Some advice does fit in a sentence: put your hands out in front, with your wrist guards on, to keep your head off the ground. Most people do it by instinct anyway. What doesn't fit in a sentence is how to go loose in the middle of a fall. Staying relaxed is good advice; knowing when, while it happens, comes from having fallen before.

So read what the professionals say below, and use it. If it doesn't work the first few times, you are not doing it wrong. That is what a first fall is like.

What the professionals do: the same idea, as a drill

Skate makers and coaches start where I do. Riedell's guide for beginners says it plainly: “Start slow and expect to fall.” Where they go further is in how they get you through those first falls: they have you fall on purpose, before it happens by accident.

Roller derby builds its training on it. In a Smithsonian Folklife article on roller derby, Gabrielle Puglisi, who skates with Charm City Roller Derby, writes: “The first thing you learn in roller derby is how to fall.” The coach blows the whistle, everyone goes down, gets back up, and goes down again. Peach State Roller Derby's training for new skaters sums it up: “Repetition is your friend.” And the WFTDA's beginner curriculum, which derby leagues use to build their training, puts protecting your body in a fall among the very first skills, next to wearing full gear, and says beginners should have “the space to fail and the support to try again.”

Put together, the drill looks like this:

  • Slowly, from standing still. Inmove's guide to falling is direct: “First, practice stationary falls, then increase your speed.” It says the point of falling under control is to build the muscle memory you use when the fall is real.
  • On something soft. KidsHealth's inline skating safety page, reviewed by a doctor, tells beginners to “practice falling on a soft surface” and suggests grass as a place to learn. It also says to crouch as you go down, so you have less far to fall.
  • In full gear, from the first try.
  • Never alone, and with a teacher or somewhere supervised if you can. That one is my rule, at any age, and above all if you are learning outside rather than in a supervised place: alone, your first falls can be really dangerous.
  • Again and again, until going down forward is what your body does without asking.

The quad version on this site comes from Najete Kada, a French champion in artistic roller skating who teaches quad beginners in her Tuto roller videos. She turns the fall into a slow descent: from the safety position, knees bent and hands on your knees, she squats all the way to the floor, puts her hands down, then stretches her legs out in front of her. She repeats it on camera, standing still, again and again.

Roces, in its guide to falling, tells you to “avoid stiffening up”, and KidsHealth says the same. That is the relaxing I said you can't learn from a paragraph. The drill is how it gets into your body.

None of this contradicts what I said above. It is the same idea seen from the other side: the drill is a way to live through your first falls on purpose, at a speed where they cost little. It does not promise you will fall well at speed. Nothing can.

The gear that takes the fall while the reflex isn't there yet

Until the reflex is there, your pads do the work. At the start, every piece matters and none of them is optional:

  • Wrist guards take the forward fall. Putting a bare palm down at speed is how wrists break.
  • Elbow pads take the backward fall: going backwards, they are what you catch yourself on.
  • Knee pads let you go down forward on purpose instead of fighting to stay up. KidsHealth and Inmove both tell you to slide on your knee pads and wrist guards when you fall forward.
  • A helmet you wear a hundred times for nothing and once for everything.

This is not a ranked list; a list has to be written in some order. Later, once skating is routine, what you wear is your call. At the start, it is all of it. The full list, with what each piece is for, is in your first pads: you need all four.

What this page does not cover: falling at speed, on a hill or in traffic, and what to do about an injury. It is not medical advice.

Quick answers

How do you fall on roller skates?

Forwards when you can, onto your knee pads and wrist guards, with your hands out in front to keep your head off the ground. But expect your first falls not to go to plan: a fall is too fast to follow steps, and the first time you panic. Learn it the way coaches teach it: on purpose, slowly, on a soft floor, in full gear, never alone, many times.

Why don't falling tips work when I actually fall?

Because a fall is over too fast to decide anything, and a first fall comes with panic. Tips read beforehand rarely survive that. What changes it is having been through falls already, small and slow ones while you learn, so that the fear is something you know.

How do you practise falling on roller skates?

Start standing still, on grass or carpet, in full gear, with someone nearby. Go down forward onto your knee pads, slowly, then do it again. Coaches and derby leagues repeat it until it is a reflex, and only then add speed, a little at a time.

What protective gear do you need for falling?

All of it from day one: helmet, wrist guards, elbow pads and knee pads. Wrist guards take the forward fall, elbow pads the backward one, and knee pads let you go down forward on purpose. It is not a ranked list.
