How Do You Go Downhill on Roller Skates?

Written 2 November 2026 · by Cass · written with an AI assistant

There are two answers, and which one applies depends on the hill. On a gentle slope you practise a controlled stop — ICP-certified coach Asha Kirkby teaches a downhill plough with your knees over your toes, and a downhill T-stop with most of your weight on the front leg. On a hill that is genuinely steep, my answer as a street skater is different and it is the one that matters most: you do not brake. You keep the wheels straight, bend your knees, make no movement you do not need, and commit to the descent until it flattens out. Trying to brake in the middle of a real hill is the dangerous move.

Should you brake going downhill on roller skates?

On a real hill, no. The steeper the slope, the more every gesture becomes a risk, and a stop is a gesture that turns a wheel across your direction of travel. On a slope that has become steep, that is more likely to put you on the ground than to slow you. The right move is to assume the descent: do not panic, keep the wheels as straight as you can, bend your knees, feel the tarmac under your feet, and wait — without tiring yourself — for the slope to ease. At the bottom, when it flattens, you can start braking.

How do you slow down on a gentle slope?

With a stop you already trust on the flat. That is the condition: one reliable flat stop before any slope at all. Then, on an easy gradient, Asha Kirkby's downhill versions apply — a plough where your knees must cover your toes so your weight stays forward, or a T-stop with about eighty percent of you on the front leg and the back skate grazing rather than digging. Both are on the pages below. On an inline skate with a heel brake, she also teaches sitting down into the stance and pushing the brake forward on a hill; I use that brake very little myself, and the page on it gives both opinions.

How steep is too steep?

I cannot give you a number, and I will not invent one. I do not know how to read a gradient by eye, and I would rather say so than put a figure on a page that could get someone hurt. The rule works in feelings instead: a descent where you doubt you could stop is already a descent you commit to rather than brake on. And the real danger is never the slope itself — it is rolling into one you have not measured, then finding out halfway that you cannot stop. Even if you have learned to brake, the first few times under stress you will panic.

Should I lean forward or stay upright on a hill?

Knees bent, back slightly forward — but do not crouch deep if you are scared. Crouching lower makes you faster. How low to go is a judgement you make yourself, and it depends on how long the hill is, so it is not a fixed instruction. What stays fixed is the rest: wheels straight, knees soft, no unnecessary movement.

How should a beginner practise hills?

Tiny ones, short ones, very little height — and even those, committed to rather than braked. For a beginner, assuming a small descent is usually the better behaviour, because it builds the one reflex that saves you later: not panicking. The slope you would not even notice on foot is the one that catches people, which is why a beginner starts on gradients that barely count.

What this page does not cover: this page is about ordinary road and path gradients. It is not about skatepark ramps or bowls, downhill racing, or skating on wet slopes, and it does not give a degree of slope as a threshold, because I cannot measure one by eye.
