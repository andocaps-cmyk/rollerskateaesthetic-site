How Do You Get Over the Fear of Roller Skating?

Written 2 November 2026 · updated 13 November 2026 · by Cass · written with an AI assistant

Start by separating what is actually dangerous from what only feels it. The wobble on inline skates is fear, not the skate: a rigid boot locks your ankle and the wheels become an extension of your leg, which is more stable than it looks. The forward fall is the one you can practise until it stops being scary. What deserves respect is different: a hill you did not measure, panic, traffic, wet painted lines. Fear of the right things keeps you safe; fear of the wrong things keeps you upright and stiff, which is how beginners fall.

Why do I feel so unstable on roller skates?

On inline skates, because you are afraid, not because the skate is. The floating feeling is psychological. The boot is a rigid shell; done up firmly, it holds the ankle so that the skate and your leg move as one thing. Standing on a single line of wheels looks terrifying and feels, once you trust it, shockingly solid. The fix is in the fastenings and the knees, not in your nerves: do the boot up properly, bend more than feels natural, and the instability mostly goes away on its own.

How do I stop being scared of falling?

Practise falling forward, slowly, in full pads. The safe fall is forward, on knee pads and wrist guards; you see it coming and you can even roll through it. Practise it on carpet or grass, in full gear, from standing, and never alone. Keep it slow: the point is to have been through a few falls at walking pace before you ever fall at speed. No practice makes a fall at speed safe. The dangerous fall is backwards, and fighting to stay upright is exactly what causes it — so the basic advice is the opposite of what fear tells you: when you lose it, go down forward, do not straighten up.

What should I actually be afraid of?

Rolling into a hill you have not measured. People see a slope, go for it, and find out halfway that they cannot stop; even if they know how to brake, the first times under stress they panic. I have seen someone drive into a car out of panic. That fear is useful: start on slopes that barely count, learn one stop you trust on the flat, and treat the city centre as closed until your stops and turns are perfect. The feelings that make this sport worth it come with danger — I believe you cannot have one without the other — and the way to earn them is to respect the right things.

Does the fear go away with time?

The fear of the skate does. The respect for the hill should not. Once your stops are reliable and you have fallen forward enough times to know what it is, the wobble and the dread of the first push fade. What stays, and should, is the judgement: do not panic, keep the wheels straight, bend the knees, and never enter a descent you have not looked at. A jump is a choice; a hill is not.

I had a bad fall and I am scared to go back. What now?

Your head is ready before your body is, and that is normal. I cannot tell you my own way back after a big fall — it is not something I have written down — but ICP-certified coach Asha Kirkby has two pages on it below: the body remembers the fall your head has already filed away, and the aim is to come back a better skater than the one who fell, not just the same one. Go back to flat ground, full pads, one stop, and let the body catch up.

What this page does not cover: this page is about beginner fear on flat ground and on the first small slopes. It is not about anxiety as a medical matter, it does not cover coming back from an injury that needed treatment, and it is not about ramps or jumps.
